## Problem

If
\[
\bigl(f(x)\bigr)^{2}=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{t f(t)}{1+t^{2}}\,dt,
\]
compute \(\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)\,dx\). The original page supplies no positivity or nonzero condition on \(f\).

## Graded clarification

For this catalog row, \(f\) is real and differentiable on \([-1,1]\), and
\[
f(x)>0\qquad\text{for every }0<|x|\leq1.
\]
The equation itself gives \(f(0)=0\). This bilateral positivity condition selects the nontrivial branch on **both** sides of zero. It is a problem clarification, recorded in the catalog as well as here, that makes the marked answer unique. The printed statement without this condition remains underdetermined; its zero and one-sided solutions are not the graded object.

## Source conflict

The function \(f_0(x)=0\) satisfies the printed equation everywhere and gives integral \(0\). The smooth function
\[
f_1(x)=\frac14\log(1+x^2)
\]
also satisfies it: both sides vanish at \(x=0\), and
\[
\frac{d}{dx}f_1(x)^2
=\frac{x\log(1+x^2)}{4(1+x^2)}
=\frac{x f_1(x)}{1+x^2}.
\]
Its integral is
\[
\int_{-1}^{1}f_1(x)\,dx
=\frac{\pi+\log 4-4}{4}>0,
\]
which agrees with the printed mark. These different values arise from two valid solutions of the same printed equation.

Merely saying that \(f\) is differentiable and nontrivial near zero is also insufficient. For example,
\[
g(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,&x\leq0,\\
\frac14\log(1+x^2),&x>0
\end{cases}
\]
is continuously differentiable: both the function and its first derivative tend to zero at the joining point. It satisfies the printed equation separately on each side, but gives
\[
\int_{-1}^{1}g(x)\,dx=\frac{\pi+\log4-4}{8}.
\]
It is nonzero in every two-sided neighborhood of zero. The clarified positivity condition excludes both \(f_0\) and \(g\), including the reflected one-sided example.

## Uniqueness under the clarification

Differentiating the integral equation gives
\[
2f(x)f'(x)=\frac{x f(x)}{1+x^2}.
\]
For \(x\ne0\), the clarified hypothesis permits cancellation of \(f(x)\). Thus
\[
f'(x)=\frac{x}{2(1+x^2)}.
\]
On the two intervals \((-1,0)\) and \((0,1)\), respectively, integration yields
\[
f(x)=\frac14\log(1+x^2)+C_-,\qquad
f(x)=\frac14\log(1+x^2)+C_+.
\]
Differentiability implies continuity at zero, and \(f(0)=0\), so both constants vanish. The resulting \(f_1\) is positive whenever \(x\ne0\), since \(1+x^2>1\). It therefore satisfies the clarification and is its unique solution.

## Integral-equation certificate and final value

Set
\[
H(x)=\frac1{16}\log^2(1+x^2).
\]
Then \(H(0)=0\), \(H(x)=f_1(x)^2\), and
\[
H'(x)=\frac{x f_1(x)}{1+x^2}.
\]
The fundamental theorem of calculus certifies the original integral equation for this branch on the whole interval, including negative \(x\).

An antiderivative of \(f_1\) is
\[
A(x)=\frac{x\log(1+x^2)-2x+2\arctan x}{4}.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-1}^{1}f_1(x)\,dx
&=A(1)-A(-1)\\
&=\frac{\pi+2\log2-4}{4}
=\boxed{\frac{\pi+\log4-4}{4}}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The encoded definite integral is \(\int_{-1}^{1}\log(1+x^2)/4\,dx\), together with the explicit branch hypothesis in the catalog. The mark is accepted for this clarified problem; it is not asserted to follow uniquely from the unamended PDF.

## SymPy self-check

SymPy verifies \(f_1(0)=H(0)=0\), \(H=f_1^2\), \(H'=xf_1/(1+x^2)\), and \(A'=f_1\), then evaluates \(A(1)-A(-1)\) to the printed mark. Direct symbolic integration independently gives the same value and verifies the integral equation. The zero final and the half-mark from the one-sided branch are wrong-answer controls under the clarified grading hypothesis. The uniqueness argument above explains why those valid solutions of the unamended print are excluded.
