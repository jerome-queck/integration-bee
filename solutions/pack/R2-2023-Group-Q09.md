## Problem

For a nonnegative integer \(n\),

\[
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\binom{n}{x}\,dx.
\]

## Solution

The binomial coefficient extends by \(\binom{n}{x}=\Gamma(n+1)/(\Gamma(x+1)\Gamma(n-x+1))\). The reflection formula \(\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\pi/\sin(\pi z)\) and the Pochhammer identity \(\Gamma(n-x+1)=(1-x)(2-x)\cdots(n-x)\Gamma(1-x)\) give, for \(n\geq 1\),

\[
\binom{n}{x}=\frac{n!\,\sin(\pi x)}{\pi\, x(1-x)(2-x)\cdots(n-x)}.
\]

The denominator has degree \(n+1\geq 2\), so the integrand is \(O(|x|^{-2})\) at infinity and the integral converges absolutely. For \(n=0\) the same reflection leaves \(\sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)\), whose integral over the line is \(1\).

Pascal's identity \(\binom{n}{x}=\binom{n-1}{x}+\binom{n-1}{x-1}\) holds for the gamma extension. For \(n=1\),

\[
\binom{1}{x}=\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x}+\frac{\sin(\pi(1-x))}{\pi(1-x)}.
\]

Each improper integral equals \(1\), so the \(n=1\) integral equals \(2\). For \(n\geq 2\) both summands are absolutely integrable, and translating the second one does not change its integral, so \(I_n=2I_{n-1}\). Therefore \(I_n=2^n\). The mark scheme prints \(2^n\).

## Final answer

\[
2^n.
\]
