## Problem

\[
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin x\,\sin(\sin x)}{x^{2}}\,e^{\cos x}\,dx.
\]

## Solution

The factor \(e^{\cos x}\) multiplies the numerator. The imaginary part of \(e^{e^{ix}}\) is \(e^{\cos x}\sin(\sin x)\), and
\[
e^{e^{ix}}=\sum_{n\ge 0}\frac{e^{inx}}{n!},
\]
so
\[
e^{\cos x}\sin(\sin x)=\sum_{n\ge 1}\frac{\sin(nx)}{n!}.
\]
The integral is
\[
\sum_{n\ge 1}\frac{1}{n!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin x\sin(nx)}{x^{2}}\,dx.
\]
This interchange is absolute: the magnitude of the term without \(1/n!\) is at most \(n\) on \((0,1]\), and at most \(x^{-2}\) on \([1,\infty)\). Both resulting factorial sums converge.
The product-to-sum identity and
\[
\cos((n-1)x)-\cos((n+1)x)=\int_{n-1}^{n+1}x\sin(yx)\,dy
\]
turn each term into
\[
\int_{n-1}^{n+1}\frac12\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(yx)}{x}\,dx\,dy.
\]
For \(y>0\), the inner Dirichlet integral equals \(\pi/2\) (insert \(e^{-\varepsilon x}\) and let \(\varepsilon\downarrow 0\) to justify the switch). Every \(n\ge 1\) therefore contributes \(\pi/2\), and
\[
\sum_{n\ge 1}\frac{1}{n!}=e-1
\]
multiplies that contribution to give \(\pi(e-1)/2\).

## Final answer

\[
\dfrac{\pi(e-1)}{2}.
\]

## SymPy self-check

SymPy sums \(\sum_{n\ge 1}1/n!=e-1\) and evaluates \(\int_{0}^{\infty}(\sin t)/t\,dt=\pi/2\). For each integer \(n\ge 1\), \((n+1)-|n-1|=2\), which is the length that produces one factor of \(\pi/2\).
